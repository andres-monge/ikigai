# How To Figure Out What To Work On

The answer to "what should I work on next?" is not inside you waiting to be found, it has to be created by doing things.

Most career advice assumes that your calling is already somewhere inside you, and that if you do enough soul-searching, it will be revealed to you.

Reflection can help by suggesting possibilities, but is only the first step. It's through doing things that you learn about yourself, and it's this self-knowledge that will let you evaluate the possible paths.

## The problem

Most of us pick a career path when we're young and have no idea what different types of work are like.

Almost 70% of employees report being disengaged at work. 25% would choose an entirely different occupation if they could start over. This problem will only grow as AI transforms the job market. More people will have to answer “what should I work on next?” not once, but repeatedly throughout their lives.

I've already had to answer it twice.

## Why I want to tackle the problem

Like most people, I had no idea what I wanted to do after school. Following my mother's expectations, I studied business at a prestigious university. After I finished my degree, I continued to go with the flow, which led me to investment banking.

Once I had to actually endure investment banking and it stopped being an intangible idea, it sucked so much I realized it was time to grab the steering wheel.

That was the first step, but the main problem remained. I clearly knew what I *didn't* want to do, but still had no idea of what I *wanted* to do.

I followed my curiosity and worked on an eclectic mix of side projects, from learning to code (and failing) to making t-shirts. I made an effort to be honest about what gave me energy and I actually enjoyed doing, and discard what was initially appealing because of money, prestige or expectations.

I had always been the music guy in my friend group, but making a living from that sounded as plausible as being a magician. I started a music blog and noticed I really enjoyed doing it, even when I came home tired from work. Eventually, I managed to make the unlikely jump from investment banking to music curation and landed my dream job.

But in 2024 I began to see that AI was going to make my job irrelevant, and I found myself going through the process of figuring out what to work on once again. At least I knew how to navigate it this time round. 

Although it was discovering ChatGPT that made me see my job wouldn't last long, it was also a "holy shit" moment. I started building stuff for myself, then building AI tools for teams I knew at work. One project led to another and I used that experience to have an Applied AI role created for me inside my company.

## What I learned

When you do things, the unknowns of a possible path reveal themselves:

How deep is your interest? Does it energize you? Are you willing to put in the effort? Do you like doing it every day, or only the idea of being the person who does it? What are the people in the field like?

Only then will you have enough information to decide whether you'd like to continue exploring a path or try a different one. 

This is why the two most common forms of career advice are wrong:

1) Traditional career guidance focuses on where your experience and skills might be valued, without taking fulfilment and fit into account. It also ignores that you can just develop new skills, and that the job that you could end up doing might not exist yet.
   
2) Follow your passion assumes you already know what your passion is, which in most cases is not true. And it assumes you can get the answer with some soul searching without actually doing the thing. If you dream about quitting your office job to be a movie director, you should try making a short film first. 

The highest chance of success comes from following your curiosity to work on your own projects, pay attention to what gives you energy, and use what you learn to choose the next one. The point of a project is not necessarily to succeed, it's to learn if it's something you'd want to pursue.

Think of it like dating rather than picking a destination. Projects are dates: you start with something chill that is low commitment, then invest more only if each date makes you want another.

I'm not the first person to suggest running low-stakes experiments, but I believe existing approaches aren't the best way to tackle this problem:

Books can offer useful methods (e.g. "Design Your Life" and "Runnin' Down a Dream" are great), but aren't personalized to your situation, and it's on you to turn them into experiments and follow through with the loop alone without guidance.

Courses are expensive, do not scale, and can start you on your journey but not guide you through it. Coaches can help with this, but are prohibitively expensive.

## The solution

A personalized guide to walk you through every step of the way that doesn't cost a fortune.

- Guiding self-reflection
- Suggesting possible paths to explore
- Designing personalized projects to work on for any given path 
- Translating results into high quality self-knowledge
- Finding peers in the area of interest
- Deciding when to stop exploring and commit
- Using the body of work you already have under your belt to help you start your new career path
