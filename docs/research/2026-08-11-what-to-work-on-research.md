# Figuring Out What to Work On: Research Synthesis

**Date:** 2026-08-11

**Status:** Point-in-time research synthesis

**Product interpretation:** [What-to-work-on product direction](./2026-08-11-what-to-work-on-product-direction.md)

> Historical snapshot. This records the current research synthesis, not current product direction. It distills the useful findings from the Deep Research conversations; the raw AI reports are not canonical sources.

## The Problem

“Figuring out what to work on” means finding a field, problem, or craft in which a person has enough curiosity to keep going, can develop unusual ability, and may eventually do important work. It is not the narrower task of matching a person to a known occupation, and it is not a request to identify orphaned social problems.

The meta-problem may itself be orphaned. Career guidance, vocational psychology, education, life design, and biographies of exceptional people each address part of it, but no established field appears responsible for delivering the whole outcome: helping a particular person move from uncertainty to a direction grounded in lived evidence.

## Executive Synthesis

The most promising answer is not a better one-time recommendation. It is a better discovery process.

Reflection can generate plausible directions, but some of the most important self-knowledge does not exist before action. People learn what holds their attention, which difficulties they will tolerate, what they can become good at, and which environments suit them by doing representative work and observing what happens.

The best current process is therefore:

1. Generate several plausible directions.
2. Turn each direction into an explicit hypothesis.
3. Run a small experiment that resembles the real work.
4. Observe curiosity, energy, learning, performance, and environmental fit.
5. Update the hypothesis and either deepen, alter, or drop it.
6. Make a provisional commitment when one direction has accumulated enough evidence.
7. Reassess as the person and the field change.

Designing Your Life provides the strongest existing practical protocol for the first five steps. Bill Gurley’s *Runnin’ Down a Dream* adds a valuable signal—revealed fascination with the work itself—but not yet a tested discovery method. Vocational and interest research explains why action can change both self-knowledge and the interests being measured. The main opportunity is to combine these contributions into a personalized, adaptive loop and test whether it produces better decisions over time.

## The Most Useful Contributions

### Paul Graham: curiosity, aptitude, and the edge of knowledge

Paul Graham’s [“How to Do Great Work”](https://www.paulgraham.com/greatwork.html) offers the clearest framing of the outcome. Choose a field, learn enough to reach its frontier, notice gaps, and pursue the ones that seem unusually interesting. Curiosity matters because great work requires sustained effort; aptitude matters because interest alone does not guarantee contribution. The field can be revised as understanding grows.

The practical implication is that direction is discovered through motion. A person cannot reason all the way to an answer from outside a field. They must try things, learn enough to see what the work is actually like, and follow questions that continue to pull them.

This is a powerful orientation, but not a complete intervention. It says what a productive search looks like more clearly than it specifies how to help a person conduct that search.

### Designing Your Life: the best current exploration protocol

Stanford’s [Life Design Lab](https://lifedesignlab.stanford.edu/dyl) applies design thinking to life and career decisions. Its most useful elements are:

- Reframe the decision as a process rather than a single correct answer.
- Generate multiple “Odyssey Plans” instead of prematurely optimizing one future.
- Identify assumptions behind each possible direction.
- Prototype with conversations and experiences before making a large commitment.
- Treat what happens as feedback, then iterate.

The core strength is epistemic: a prototype is not merely a cautious first step; it is a way to obtain information that introspection cannot supply. Stanford’s [workbook](https://lifedesignlab.stanford.edu/s/June-2024-Virtual-Studio-Workbook.pdf) explicitly treats prototypes as tests of assumptions and sources of new insight.

The direct evidence is encouraging but limited. Lindsay Oishi’s [2012 dissertation](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Enhancing-career-development-agency-in-emerging-adulthood-%3A-an-intervention-using-design-thinking/oclc/794542443) found increased career-development self-efficacy and moderately reduced negative affect after a design-thinking course, while uncertainty did not significantly change. Timothy Reilly’s [2013 dissertation](https://coa.stanford.edu/publications/designing-life-studies-emerging-adult-development) reported gains in areas including meaningfulness, growth, and life decision-making. These studies support proximal benefits such as agency and confidence; they do not establish that the approach produces better long-term fit or exceptional work.

Designing Your Life is therefore the best practical baseline, not a finished or strongly validated solution.

### Bill Gurley: revealed fascination with the practice

Bill Gurley’s [*Runnin’ Down a Dream*](https://runnindownadream.com/) is based on a long study of people who pursued their work with unusual intensity. Its most important contribution is a sharper interpretation of passion.

The useful signal is not whether someone admires an outcome, identity, or successful person. It is whether they are repeatedly drawn to the underlying practice: the preparation, technical details, history, tools, conversations, and unglamorous work. Call this **revealed fascination**. It is closer to observed behavior than to a declared preference.

This improves on prompts such as “What are you passionate about?” A person may want the status of being a filmmaker while disliking editing, or like the idea of entrepreneurship while avoiding customers. Conversely, they may spend hours on an activity without yet treating it as a possible direction. The distinction between attraction to the outcome and attraction to the work is highly useful for both reflection and experiment design.

The limitation is evidence. Gurley’s cases are hypothesis-generating biographies selected from people who became highly accomplished. They are vulnerable to survivor bias and do not show that the same signals can reliably guide people earlier in the process. The book should inform questions to test, not become a scoring system.

Gurley is also better treated as an important source than as the enduring owner of this problem. The [Runnin’ Down a Dream Foundation](https://rdad.org/) is currently a micro-grant and mentoring program for US adults who have read the book and are already pursuing a dream. Its stated intervention is support after a direction has been found, not helping an uncertain person choose what to work on. The depth of Gurley’s continuing involvement and the program’s longer-term direction remain unclear.

### Vocational and interest research: interests are partly developed through action

The research base complicates the idea that a person has one pre-existing passion waiting to be uncovered.

Hidi and Renninger’s [four-phase model of interest development](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_4) describes movement from triggered situational interest through maintained interest to an increasingly stable individual interest. O’Keefe, Dweck, and Walton’s [research on theories of interest](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6180666/) found that a fixed “find your passion” belief can reduce openness to new areas and make normal difficulty look like evidence of poor fit.

Career-development research also treats exploration as consequential rather than incidental. The [meta-analysis of career exploration by Kleine, Schmitt, and Wisse](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2021.103645) synthesizes its relationships with antecedents and career outcomes. [Social Cognitive Career Theory](https://doi.org/10.1006/jvbe.1994.1027) explains how interests and choices interact with self-efficacy, expected outcomes, goals, supports, and barriers.

Work on career transitions and identity, including Herminia Ibarra’s [*Working Identity*](https://herminiaibarra.com/working-identity-book/), reinforces action-first exploration. It does not add a distinct mechanism beyond this broader vocational and interest literature, so it should remain supporting context rather than a separate approach.

The implication is important: the system should not merely recover a hidden preference. It should help a person discover and develop interests while distinguishing transient novelty from increasing attachment to a practice.

## What Appears Supported—and What Does Not Yet

| Claim | Current assessment |
|---|---|
| Introspection alone is insufficient; experience supplies new information | Strong practical and theoretical convergence; the contribution of action has not been isolated in the cited evaluations |
| Interests can develop rather than simply wait to be discovered | Supported by interest-development research |
| A Designing Your Life course can improve agency-related proximal outcomes | Limited course-level evidence from small, early studies; the effects of multiple possibilities and prototypes were not tested separately |
| Supports, barriers, self-efficacy, and context influence viable directions | Established in vocational psychology |
| Attraction to the actual practice is more informative than attraction to its status | Strong working hypothesis; compelling case evidence, not yet a validated predictor |
| A repeated adaptive loop will outperform a one-time recommendation | Central product hypothesis; not yet established |
| These methods lead to durable fit, exceptional performance, or “great work” | Unknown; long-term comparative evidence is missing |

## Important Tensions

### Discovery versus development

Some signals reveal an existing pattern; others are created by exposure, competence, and encouragement. A useful process has to do both: notice what is already present without treating current interests as fixed limits.

### Exploration versus commitment

More experiments are not always better. Endless option generation can become avoidance, while premature commitment can lock in a poorly tested direction. The process needs stopping rules and provisional commitments, not permanent certainty.

### Productive difficulty versus genuine misfit

Difficulty may be evidence of growth, poor instruction, an unsupportive environment, or a bad fit. A single reaction to a hard task is ambiguous. Interpretation should consider whether curiosity returns, capability grows, and the person wants another attempt.

### Novelty versus durable fascination

Initial excitement is cheap. A stronger signal is recurring voluntary engagement after novelty fades, especially with the detailed and inconvenient parts of the work.

### Experiment convenience versus fidelity

An experiment must be small enough to run but representative enough to teach. Watching videos about medicine tests interest in medicine content, not interest in clinical work. Every prototype should state which assumption it tests and which parts of the real practice it reproduces.

## Frontier Questions

1. Which observed signals best predict that a person will want to deepen a direction six or twelve months later?
2. How should signals such as curiosity, energy, skill growth, contribution, social fit, and constraint fit be combined without manufacturing false precision?
3. How much real-work fidelity is required for an experiment to be informative?
4. When should the process encourage persistence through difficulty, and when should it recommend switching?
5. Can an AI reliably interpret reflection and behavioral evidence without narrowing a person to its prior assumptions?
6. What is the right cadence for divergence, experimentation, and provisional commitment at different life stages?
7. Does an adaptive sequence of experiments improve agency and decision quality more than a static report or ordinary counseling?
8. What outcomes should define success: confidence, action, sustained engagement, skill growth, wellbeing, contribution, or some combination over time?

## People and Institutions Worth Tracking

- **Bill Burnett, Dave Evans, and the Stanford Life Design Lab:** creators of the most complete active, teachable exploration practice and the best practical baseline. Start with the [Life Design resources and exercises](https://lifedesignlab.stanford.edu/resources-1).
- **Bill Gurley and the Runnin’ Down a Dream Foundation:** a valuable source on revealed fascination and a potential source of downstream evidence about committed pursuit. The current grant program does not own the earlier discovery problem; monitor whether its program or evidence base expands.
- **Bryan Dik and the LIVE Project:** Dik leads a new longitudinal investigation of vocational exploration among young adults, making it especially relevant to the missing long-term evidence. Start with his [Colorado State University research profile](https://newsmediarelations.colostate.edu/contacts/bryan-dik/).
- **Andreas Hirschi:** an active career-development researcher connecting career choice, self-management, resources, and changing labor markets. Start with his [University of Bern research profile and selected publications](https://www.aop.psy.unibe.ch/ueber_uns/personen/ahirschi/).

The field does not yet appear to have a single clear owner. That gap is part of the opportunity, but it is too early to claim ownership before showing that a combined approach works.
