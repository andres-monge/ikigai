# What-to-Work-On Product Direction

**Date:** 2026-08-11

**Status:** Point-in-time product hypothesis

**Research basis:** [Figuring out what to work on](./2026-08-11-what-to-work-on-research.md)

> Historical snapshot. This proposes what the research may mean for Revelio. It does not supersede the current code or establish current product direction without real-user validation.

## Recommendation

Revelio should explore a shift from **generating an answer about what to work on** to **helping a person run a better discovery process**.

The product thesis is:

> Reflection generates possibilities. Action generates self-knowledge.

The immediate consequence is not a large build. The next step should be a manual, longitudinal pilot that tests whether returning after a real-world experiment creates substantially more value than the existing one-time experience.

## The User Outcome

The product should not promise certainty or identify a single correct career. It should help a user leave each interaction with:

1. A small number of plausible directions they understand.
2. A clear hypothesis about why each might fit.
3. One feasible, representative next experiment.
4. A way to notice and interpret what the experiment taught them.
5. A justified decision to deepen, alter, or drop the direction.

The durable outcome is **greater agency and better evidence for the next decision**, not a definitive label.

## Proposed Discovery Loop

### 1. Ideate

Use reflection to generate multiple possibilities. Draw on the person’s curiosity, recurring behavior, abilities, values, constraints, admired work, and unexplained fascinations. Avoid presenting current interests as fixed boundaries.

### 2. Make the hypotheses explicit

For each direction, say why it may fit and what remains unknown. A useful path is falsifiable: “You may enjoy investigative journalism because you repeatedly pursue unanswered questions” is more useful than “You are a natural storyteller.”

### 3. Prototype the practice

Choose a small activity that resembles the real work, not its image or surrounding content. State which assumption the experiment tests. Prefer reversible steps that can be completed soon and produce observable evidence.

### 4. Observe

After the experiment, collect a short account of what happened. Potential signals include:

- Did curiosity return after the novelty wore off?
- Which parts produced or drained energy?
- Did the user voluntarily go beyond the assignment?
- Did capability or understanding grow?
- Which difficulty felt absorbing, and which felt merely aversive?
- Did the people, setting, and constraints fit?
- Does the user want a more demanding version of the work?

These are prompts for judgment, not a validated score.

### 5. Update

Compare the observation with the original hypothesis. Deepen the direction, modify it, or drop it. Preserve the evidence so the next recommendation reflects what the person has actually done rather than restarting from a questionnaire.

### 6. Commit provisionally

When evidence accumulates, help the user choose a longer project, course, role, or field of study for a defined period. Commitment is necessary to get beyond shallow sampling; it need not be treated as permanent.

## The Distinctive Product Hypothesis

Designing Your Life already supplies a strong general protocol for divergent planning and prototyping. Revelio would need to improve on a book, workbook, or counselor-led exercise through personalization and continuity:

- Translate a person’s reflection into testable hypotheses.
- Design experiments that fit their actual age, resources, location, and constraints.
- Make each experiment representative of the underlying practice.
- Remember prior experiments and adapt the next one.
- Help interpret ambiguous evidence without pretending to have a scientific fit score.
- Notice revealed fascination: attraction to the practice and its details, not only to the outcome or identity.

This is the unproven center of the product. If adaptation across repeated interactions does not produce meaningful additional value, the product is mostly a polished implementation of existing life-design exercises.

## Relationship to the Current Product

The current application already contains useful parts of this model:

- `server/ai/prompts.ts` validates uncertainty and says that trying things is how a student figures out what to work on.
- It produces exactly three paths, creating some deliberate divergence.
- The action-plan prompt frames early milestones as low-risk experiments intended to teach whether a path is right.

There is also a conceptual contradiction. The purpose-discovery prompt says each path reveals “something they already know but haven’t articulated.” The research suggests that some of the decisive information is not hidden inside the user; it is generated by experience. The current flow therefore ends just as the most informative part should begin:

`questionnaire → three paths → choose one → action plan → export`

The hypothesized future loop is:

`reflection → three hypotheses → representative experiment → observation → update → next experiment or provisional commitment`

The current data model in `shared/streaming-schemas.ts` represents paths, milestones, and an action plan. It does not represent hypotheses, experiment evidence, or changes in confidence over time. That is a future implementation implication, not a reason to change the schema before validation.

## Next Validation: Run It Manually

Use the next real-user pilot to test one complete loop without building a new system.

1. Present each generated path as a hypothesis rather than a recommendation.
2. Ask the user to choose one path worth learning more about.
3. Co-design one small, high-fidelity experiment they can complete within one or two weeks.
4. Record the hypothesis and what the experiment is intended to test.
5. Hold a short follow-up after the user completes or abandons it.
6. Capture what they did, what surprised them, which parts drew them in, which difficulties appeared, and whether they want a deeper version.
7. Recommend deepen, alter, or drop, and agree on the next step.

The founder can run the hypothesis, experiment design, and follow-up manually around the existing product. This is compatible with the earlier conclusion that more product work should not precede real-user evidence.

### What to compare

Compare this with the current one-time experience. The key question is not whether users like the follow-up. It is whether the second interaction changes the quality of their understanding or decision.

Useful pilot evidence includes:

- Experiment started and completed.
- A prior assumption was confirmed, rejected, or made more precise.
- The user can explain the next decision using observed evidence.
- The follow-up changes which path they pursue or how they pursue it.
- The user voluntarily continues, escalates, or requests another experiment.
- A counselor or parent sees greater decision quality than after the static report alone.

Exporting a report may remain useful, but it is not evidence that the discovery process worked.

## Decision Gates Before Building

Do not implement a longitudinal product until a small pilot shows most of the following:

1. Users complete representative experiments at a meaningful rate.
2. Follow-up observations contain information that was not available in the initial questionnaire.
3. That information materially changes or strengthens the next decision.
4. Users want another iteration rather than treating the report as the end.
5. The manual process reveals repeatable elements that software can improve.

These are build-gate categories, not yet pass/fail criteria. Before the pilot begins, record the cohort size, any comparison condition, follow-up window, and operational definitions for “meaningful completion,” “new information,” “material change,” and “wants another iteration.” Set the required counts or proportions before observing outcomes; do not derive a favorable threshold afterward.

If users do not act, first investigate experiment scope, timing, accountability, and environmental barriers. If they act but learn little, experiment fidelity or the interpretation prompts are likely weak. If they learn but do not value a second iteration, a one-time tool or counselor aid may be the better product.

## Non-Goals

- Do not claim to discover a user’s one true calling.
- Do not turn Gurley’s biographical observations into a psychometric score.
- Do not confuse this problem with helping users identify orphaned social problems.
- Do not build a comprehensive “career operating system” before testing one repeated loop.
- Do not treat confidence, satisfaction, or export rate alone as proof of better direction.
- Do not remove human judgment from ambiguous decisions; the near-term role of AI is to improve hypotheses and experiments.

## Open Product Questions

1. Which user and life stage has enough freedom to experiment and enough urgency to return?
2. What is the smallest experiment that is both feasible and representative of real work?
3. Should evidence be interpreted by the user, a counselor, the AI, or a combination?
4. What should trigger deepening versus another divergent exploration?
5. How should the product handle experiments blocked by money, access, family expectations, or school constraints?
6. How much continuity is valuable before the process feels burdensome?
7. What longer-term outcome can be measured honestly within the time available for a pilot?

Until those questions are answered with real behavior, the discovery loop should remain a product hypothesis rather than a new product architecture.
