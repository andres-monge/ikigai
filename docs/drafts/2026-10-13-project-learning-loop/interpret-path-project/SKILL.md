# Interpret What You Learned

## Purpose

Help the user understand what doing the project revealed and decide what they want to do next. Their experience informs the decision; it does not predict or decide the answer for them.

Reflection is available before completion, during work or after stopping. Do not require a finished artifact or a minimum amount of work.

## Understand the experience

For a project genuinely linked to the current path, begin a formal reflection with the English pilot question, replacing N with the actual project number:

Which parts of Path Project N made you want to keep going, and which made you question the path?

Use what the user has already told you. Focus on energy drivers and energy drainers: which parts of the actual work drew them in or wore them down, and what made them want to continue or stop.

Absorption, voluntary return, resistance, satisfaction and relevant feedback can clarify those experiences. They are useful clues, not a checklist the user must complete. Ask a follow-up only when its answer could materially change the interpretation.

Distinguish the work itself from project scope, unfamiliar tools, missing skills, external constraints or temporary circumstances. A draining experience may justify changing the approach rather than rejecting the path; enjoyable parts may coexist with difficulty. Name uncertainty when the available experience does not separate these explanations.

Relate the experience to the project's learning question. A successful result, improved skill or praise does not by itself establish that the user wants more of this work. An unfinished or unsuccessful project can still provide useful information.

Distinguish the user's reported experience from your interpretation and what remains unknown. Record supported reflection evidence through the available operations without assigning a fit score or claiming a permanent conclusion.

## Support the next decision

When the user is ready to move on, close the reflection after supported evidence from their experience is recorded. Do not keep interviewing to fill turns, or mark the project complete merely because reflection ended. If they have not tried the work, discuss their uncertainty or return to guidance without inventing reflection evidence. They can still change their mind about the project.

For experience that genuinely bears on the current path, ask the pilot question:

Do you want to continue down this path?

Respect the user's answer. They can remain uncertain, pause, ask for more context or return to practical help. Do not choose a direction for them or treat a preference as a prediction of fit.

## Decide what to change

Help the user distinguish between changing the project, changing the
path, and reconsidering their North Star.

Prefer a project adjustment when a different outcome, scope, medium
or approach would let them explore the central work they are still
interested in. Difficulty with tools, excessive scope or a disappointing
result may call for support or a smaller version rather than a new path.

Consider a path change when the user wants to explore a different kind
of work. Judge this against what the selected path actually involves;
a change of medium alone does not necessarily mean a change of path.

If the distinction is unclear, ask whether a different project would
address their concern or whether they want to try different work.
Respect a clear wish to move on without requiring further projects
or proof that the current path is wrong.

For a project adjustment, return to project development. For a path
change, return to `create-purpose-paths`, which owns reviewing the
other paths, revising the set and developing new possibilities.

If the user's experience raises doubts about the contribution they
want their work to make, discuss whether they want to reconsider their
North Star. If they do, use `form-foundation` to explore and potentially
revise it. Do not rewrite the North Star within reflection.

If they want to continue, help them distinguish exploring further with another project from committing provisionally to the path. Save a Next Move only from their explicit choice. Further discussion, research and pausing remain ordinary conversation rather than extra compulsory menu options.

An explore-further choice returns to `design-path-project` for three equal follow-on options. Provisional commitment requires confirmed, meaningful peer exposure through the peer stage; research results or suggested contacts alone do not satisfy it. Direct outreach is not required.

There is no fixed number of projects, duration, score or requirement to complete an artifact before deciding. Explain what the evidence supports and what remains uncertain, while respecting the user's informed choice.

## Intentionally unrelated projects

When the user knowingly chose a project outside their current path or North Star, reflect on that work on its own terms. It can reveal valuable energy drivers and drainers without answering the selected path's learning question.

Use natural questions about whether they want more of that kind of work. Do not ask them to affirm an inaccurate connection, record a decision about the old path from unrelated evidence, or require them to adopt a new North Star to receive help.

If they see a meaningful connection or want to revisit their direction, help them examine that choice. Keep their existing confirmed direction unchanged until they explicitly choose to revise it.
